    D major · sawtooth pads, a sine bell, a triangle drone · two crossed delays

    The piece most likely to meet you when you switch the music on, and the slowest. Pads walk at random through five chords over a low D and its fifth, each chord fading in over seven seconds and out over nine, and now and then a bell rings high up on a note of the D major pentatonic, mostly heard in its echoes. Each pad note is two sawtooth oscillators a few cents apart, through one slowly breathing low-pass filter; the echo is two delays, one each side, feeding each other, a little duller each time round.

    A minor · a pipe organ: flute, full organ and pedal stops · two crossed delays

    A pipe organ built from nothing: each stop is a waveform of a few chosen harmonics. A flute stop ticks out eight notes to the bar while full-organ chords and a pedal two octaves deep swell in under it and fall away again, over four rounds of four chords, about a minute and three quarters. Each time round picks one of two progressions, A minor, F, C, G or A minor, D minor, E. This cut goes round three times and comes home to A minor.

    The game’s sound effects · oscillators, filtered noise and a generated room

    When every hidden word in a territory has been charted, its star goes supernova. Here two go off far away, one on each side, muffled by the distance; then one right here. A drone climbs two octaves through a resonant filter, throbbing faster as it rises, and holds its breath; then the blast, a falling boom, a punch and a crack, the rumble rolling away, the shockwave rushing past from left to right and a shimmer of C major, as if the stars rang. Last come the two soft bells the game rings for an achievement: Witness, for being there when a territory goes supernova.

    E major · pulse-wave chimes, an FM bell, a triangle bass · a generated hall

    Glassy chimes rolling up and down slow arpeggios, the way an old console role-playing game sets a quiet town at night. Each chime is a narrow pulse wave, the sound 8-bit consoles made, with its highest harmonics rolled off so it rings like glass instead of buzzing. Phrases of four chords in E major, with a borrowed C or D now and then, follow one another at random, each chord broken into one of five sixteen-note figures over a soft triangle bass and a haze of held notes, and a bell made by frequency modulation sounds high above. It was the first of the pieces with a real reverb: a hall three and a half seconds long, made from noise.

    F♯ minor · piano, strings, a cello and a celesta · a generated hall

    The quiet side of a score for exploring somewhere old and dark: a slow waltz for a soft piano alone in a high, dim hall. Its tune is two phrases that leap up a sixth and fall back, the second closing on a sigh up to the minor third, and it comes back four times, a little changed each time, around a warmer middle and a darker, lower turn. Strings breathe in under it most of the way, with a cello now and then and a celesta far off. Each piano note is a few harmonics for the string, a brighter voice that dies within a second and the soft knock of the felt. It’s the one piece written to a length, the four minutes each has in the game, so here it plays once through, as written, to the rolled chord at its end.

How it’s made

Every sound here is made by Letterfield’s own code as it runs in a browser: oscillators, filters, delays and noise from the Web Audio API, with no recordings and no samples. In the game the four pieces take turns, four minutes each, crossfading, and no two plays are alike, since each draws its choices from a new seed.

For this album each piece was played from a fixed seed, through the same compressor and limiter as in the game, into an offline audio context that renders as fast as it can, and given a cut of its own with a beginning and an end. The tracks are brought to one level, −17 LUFS, and encoded as VBR MP3s at 44.1 kHz. The script that does it, render_album.py, is in the game’s repository, and makes the same album every time.
